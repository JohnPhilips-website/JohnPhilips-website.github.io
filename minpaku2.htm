Minpaku: Minpaku, the National Museum of Ethnology, is one of Japan's major centers for the ethnological study of peoples from around the world, as well as a working museum with state of the art displays of cultural artifacts from around the world. It is more than worth a day of anyone's time to browse the exhibits. It is one of the world's finest museums.

It is also one of the world's finest research institutions, with scholars from around the world. In the corridors it was easy to catch conversations in not only Japanese and English, but also Hindi, Fulfulde, Spanish and far, far more languages. The library was not only excellent but astounding, with copies of everything from the Human Relations Area Files to British Parliamentary Papers. I only wish I could have stayed longer for more research.

But while I was there I not only got the chance to do a lot of research, I met a lot of new friends, whom I'm still in touch with. Here's one of the most interesting, a Mexican-American ethnomusicologist who specializes in Japanese music, especially from Okinawa.

The Museum is sited in Senri Expo Park, a beautiful setting graced by the statue of the Sun.

The Ainu Ceremony: The Japanese National Museum of Ethnology doesn't only study ethnic groups from outside Japan, they study Japanese themselves. Most of their attention is paid to the "Japanese" Japanese, including Okinawans, and the Museum displays information on the language, material culture, beliefs, practices, and other aspects of the culture of people from around Japan. This includes Japan's other indigenous ethnic group, the Ainu. The approximately 25,000 Ainu remaining in Japan are indigenous to Hokkaido and adjacent islands in the north of Japan and nearby areas of Russia. They have a unique language. They used to live in many other areas of Japan before being pushed north by Japanese speaking agriculturalists. Many Japanese place names, especially in the northern, Tohoku region and Hokkaido, are of Ainu origin. Their traditional mode of life is that of stone-tool using hunters and gatherers, and they are in many ways the Japanese equivalent of Native Americans.

 

Shigeru Kayano is one of the most prominent Ainu leaders in Japan, and the only Ainu to have been a member of the Japanese legislature, or Diet. You can find out more about him at http://www.charlest.whipple.net/kayano.html. He came to Minpaku to perform a traditional Ainu ceremony, and the rest of us were invited to watch.
