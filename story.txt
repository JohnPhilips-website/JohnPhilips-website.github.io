I suppose it sounds from the other page either like I was a snotty, 
self-important American who insisted that Webster's spelling was right, 
or that the audience was a bunch of snotty, self-important Oxbridge twits 
who insisted that British spelling was correct, or most likely both. 

Actually it was a talk about Hausa orthography reform.  
It was intended to be a rather humorous, as well as informative, talk. 
The story I found in the archives was neither one of colonial atrocities 
nor one of the "white man's burden" but rather a very British tale of 
"always muddle through." 

I began with Marx's observation about how history always repeats itself, 
first as tragedy, then as farce. One member of the audience said later that 
he had felt embarrassed because "We're always portrayed as such a race of bumblers."  

I read all the quotes from the files in a comic parody of "marked RP" or 
"upper class twittish." (I was Freddy in my high school's production of 
G.B. Shaw's "Pygmalion" and had studied Daniel Jones' classic of English 
phonology, _The Pronunciation of English_, to get it right.) 

Actual quote from the files: "All languages are not phonetically spelled." 
(This was from a very powerful person in the colonial administration.) 
He used the example of Magdalen (pronounced "maudlin") College, Oxford. 
After the laughter died down I looked around and, in mock annoyance and
marked RP said "Well he's been to Oxford, so he knows about this sort of thing." 

One of the sillier proposals that had been made by British colonial 
administrators was to spell loan words in Hausa as they were spelled 
in the loaning language, e.g. to spell all English loanwords as if 
they were still English. Of course, doing the same in Hausa would have 
created double confusion, since the foreign loanwords in English are 
spelled as if they were still foreign. 

During the question period an African asked about that.  
It turned out that he thought the Magna Carta had been written in English
(it was in Latin), and didn't realize that spelling loanwords as they are 
spelled in the loaning language was the practice in English, even today.  

I explained the effects this practice had had on English orthography by 
using such examples [from French] as "centre" and "labour." I wrote "programme" 
on the board and said "This looks like a computer screaming for software: 
'Program me! Program me!' You don't pronounce this." I said, first circling and 
then crossing off the last two letters. "Why do you write it?" 

All the examples I used were from British spelling, no longer used in the U.S. 

Finally I switched back to the comic, marked RP accent and said 
"We don't accept the reformed orthography of our own language. 
Why should we let the natives have a reformed orthography 
for one of their languages?" 

They took it in a good humor, and one faculty member even told me later 
that the journal he was on the editorial board of accepted American spellings
"as an alternative."
